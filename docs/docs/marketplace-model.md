# Marketplace Operating Model

## Purpose

The marketplace was designed as a multi-provider travel commerce platform where products could be supplied by both internal departments and external travel providers.

The operating model was built around a simple principle:

> **One marketplace process, regardless of who owns the product.**

This meant that internal departments and external vendors followed the same core marketplace process for product submission, approval, publication, purchase, payment, notification, and fulfilment.

---

## Operating Model Principle

A second principle guided the design:

> **Centralise what needs consistency; decentralise what requires product ownership.**

The marketplace needed central governance to maintain consistency and trust, while allowing individual product owners to remain responsible for their products and fulfilment.

### Centralised Activities

The following activities were managed centrally:

* Marketplace governance
* Product approval
* Product visibility rules
* Customer checkout
* Online payment
* Marketplace-wide transaction processing
* Core reporting
* Marketplace performance monitoring

### Decentralised Activities

The following activities remained with the relevant product owner or vendor:

* Product creation
* Product-specific information
* Product availability
* Product fulfilment
* Customer servicing after purchase
* Product-specific operational decisions
* Managing fulfilment exceptions

---

## Marketplace Participants

The operating model involved five major participant groups.

### 1. Travellers / Customers

Customers interacted with the marketplace to:

* Search for travel products
* Browse product categories
* View product details
* Select products
* Add one or multiple products to their cart
* Complete payment
* Receive purchase confirmation
* Receive relevant notifications
* Receive fulfilment from the responsible vendor or internal product team

The customer experience was intentionally designed around the company's website rather than requiring customers to complete separate transactions across different provider platforms.

---

### 2. External Vendors

External travel agencies and providers could register on the marketplace and upload their own products.

Their responsibilities included:

* Registering as a marketplace vendor
* Creating products
* Providing required product information
* Uploading product images
* Specifying availability or quantity
* Submitting products for approval
* Responding to customer purchases
* Managing fulfilment
* Managing product-specific operational issues

External vendors could also pay for additional product visibility through the marketplace's boosting feature.

---

### 3. Internal Product Owners

Internal departments could also operate as marketplace vendors for products they owned.

For example, different internal teams could be responsible for:

* Curated tour packages
* Hotel-related offers
* Airport concierge services
* Other travel products

The internal product owner remained responsible for the product and its fulfilment.

The marketplace did not create a separate customer journey for internally owned products. This supported the principle that the same marketplace process should apply regardless of product ownership.

---

### 4. Marketplace Administrator

The Marketplace Administrator served as the central governance role.

The administrator reviewed submitted products against approved marketplace guidelines before publication.

The basic process was:

**Product Created → Submitted → Marketplace Administrator Review → Approved / Declined**

If approved:

**Approved → Published → Available to Customers**

If declined:

**Declined → Reason / Feedback Provided → Vendor or Product Owner Updates Product → Resubmission**

This role was deliberately separated from individual product ownership to reduce the risk of preferential treatment.

---

### 5. Executive Management

Management provided strategic oversight of marketplace performance.

Rather than focusing only on product views, management needed visibility into commercial and business outcomes such as:

* Revenue from internally uploaded products
* Revenue generated by external vendors
* Customer growth
* Customers directed to the main BTM Holidays website
* Vendor growth
* New vendor registrations
* Platform performance
* Return on investment

The executive view therefore focused on whether the marketplace was generating meaningful business value.

## Product Governance Model

A key design decision was that products would not become publicly visible immediately after submission.

Every product went through an approval process.

### Product Lifecycle

```text
Draft
  ↓
Submitted
  ↓
Under Review
  ↓
 ┌───────────────┐
 │               │
 ▼               ▼
Approved       Declined
  │               │
  ▼               ▼
Published       Feedback
  │               │
  │               ▼
  │          Product Updated
  │               │
  │               ▼
  │          Resubmitted
  │               │
  └───────←───────┘
```

This created a controlled catalogue while still allowing multiple providers to contribute products.

## Commercial Visibility Model

The marketplace supported two forms of product visibility:

### Organic Visibility

Approved products could appear normally within marketplace search and category results.

The objective was to ensure that approved products were not automatically disadvantaged because they came from an external provider or an internal department.

### Paid Boosting

Vendors and product owners could optionally pay to boost a product.

The product creation process included a visibility decision:

**Do you want to boost this product for visibility?**

If the vendor selected **Yes**, an additional fee applied.

Once approved, the boosted product received higher search placement.

Existing live products could also be boosted later if the vendor wanted additional visibility.

This created a commercial opportunity for the marketplace while maintaining a distinction between normal product visibility and paid placement.

---

## Transaction & Fulfilment Model

The marketplace separated **transaction processing** from **product fulfilment**.

The platform handled:

* Product selection
* Cart
* Checkout
* Online payment
* Purchase confirmation
* Relevant notifications
* Product quantity updates

The responsible vendor or internal product owner handled:

* Customer fulfilment
* Product delivery
* Operational coordination
* Product-specific exceptions
* Customer servicing relating to fulfilment

This allowed the platform to provide a consistent purchasing experience without requiring every product owner to build or maintain a separate payment journey.

---

## Example: Tour Product with Minimum Participants

A tour product could have a minimum participation requirement.

For example:

* Minimum participants: 10
* Booking deadline: 31 October

If the minimum number of participants was not reached by the deadline, the vendor could decide whether to proceed.

If the vendor decided not to proceed:

1. The vendor notified the platform.
2. The affected purchases were cancelled according to the applicable terms and conditions.
3. The affected traveller received a voucher equivalent to the refunded amount.
4. The voucher could be used toward another marketplace product.

This demonstrated why product-specific operational rules needed to remain with the responsible vendor while the marketplace provided the common transaction framework.

The resulting model balanced **central governance with distributed product ownership**.

| Product information         |                     | ✓                      |
| Product availability        |                     | ✓                      |
| Product boosting            | Platform capability | ✓                      |
| Customer cart               | ✓                   |                        |
| Checkout                    | ✓                   |                        |
| Online payment              | ✓                   |                        |
| Purchase notification       | ✓                   | ✓                      |
| Product fulfilment          |                     | ✓                      |

The model allowed the marketplace to scale beyond the company's own products without losing control over customer experience, catalogue quality, payment processing, and commercial governance.
